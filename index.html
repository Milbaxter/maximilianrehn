Letting Go

Everyday Practice
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Notice a difficult thought or feeling without getting pulled into it.

Letting Go is a practice Mili returns to often: noticing thoughts and feelings without holding on to them or immediately acting on them. It draws on Michael Singer's The Untethered Soul and The Surrender Experiment.

What it means

A frustrating conversation can keep playing in the mind long after it ends. Letting go means noticing that replay, allowing the discomfort to be there, and returning attention to what is happening now. The practical problem may still need a response.

Practicing it

  • Notice when the body tightens or the same thought keeps returning.
  • Give the feeling some room before deciding what to do.
  • Return to the conversation, task, or surroundings in front of you.

The aim is a habit of returning, rather than staying calm all the time.

Connections

This connects to Life Philosophy of Beauty: less time caught in an argument with reality can leave more room to notice it. Other influences include Michael Singer, Jiddu Krishnamurti, Anthony de Mello, and David R. Hawkins.

Action Over Inaction

Everyday Practice
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Start small, learn from doing, and take care with the work.

Action Over Inaction is Mili's reminder to take a useful first step when planning starts going in circles.

Starting helps

Motivation can follow action. Writing a rough paragraph or building a small prototype often makes the next step clearer. Mark Manson's “do something” principle is one influence.

Sharing unfinished work can also make embarrassment easier to handle. A small experiment gives you something real to learn from.

Making room for chance

Publishing, meeting people, and trying projects create more chances for unexpected opportunities. Mili connects this with Nassim Taleb's writing on keeping options open. There is no formula that guarantees luck.

Make haste slowly

The phrase festina lente, or “make haste slowly,” captures the balance: get moving while giving the work enough attention to do it well.

  • Choose one small step you can take today.
  • Pick a direction worth following, even if the destination is unclear.
  • Leave time to notice mistakes and improve the result.

Inner Work First

Self-understanding
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Understand your own reactions while working on problems around you.

Inner Work First is Mili's belief that understanding his own motives and reactions helps him make better choices.

Where it started

On a solo Interrail trip through Europe at twenty, he filled notebooks with ideas about what was wrong with society. Reading Jiddu Krishnamurti prompted another question: how much of his desire to change things came from frustration he had not examined?

What it means in practice

Before starting a project or entering an argument, it can help to ask what is driving it: curiosity, fear, approval, or a problem that needs solving. Mixed motives are normal; noticing them gives you more choice.

  • Meditation offers time to observe thoughts without immediately following them.
  • Reading gives personal intuitions something to be tested against.
  • Sleep, movement, and rest support the ability to pay attention.

A useful balance

Self-reflection can become another way to postpone things. This idea sits alongside Action Over Inaction: learn about yourself while participating in the world.

Create More Buddhas Per Capita

Attention & Awareness
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Mili's shorthand for helping more people understand their minds and react less automatically.

Create More Buddhas Per Capita is a phrase Mili has used in his bio. It expresses a hope that more people can learn to notice their thoughts, question their reactions, and feel less pushed around by them.

The sticky-mind metaphor

A thought can behave like Velcro: one upsetting comment catches on old worries and becomes an afternoon of replaying it. The contrasting image is Teflon, where a thought is noticed and allowed to pass. These are metaphors for a practice, not two kinds of people.

Things that can help

  1. Notice the thought. Ask whether it is a fact, an interpretation, or a worry.
  2. Practice attention. Return to the breath or a meditation practice when distracted.
  3. Zoom out. Consider how much this problem may matter with some distance.
  4. Do something useful. Give attention to a task rather than another round of brooding.
  5. Be patient. Habits take repetition to change.

Why it matters beyond the individual

Mili's broader argument is that people who can pause and question their reactions may be harder to manipulate. His essay A society asleep is a ticking time bomb explores that concern. It is a political hope behind the phrase, rather than a promise that meditation solves social problems.

Mind Constructs Reality

Perception & Beliefs
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: How expectations and habits shape the way we experience the world.

Mind Constructs Reality brings together Mili's notes on perception, beliefs, and attention. The central question is how much our expectations affect what we notice and how we interpret it.

Ideas behind the question

Source or ideaWhat Mili takes from it
Predictive processingA way of understanding perception in which the brain combines expectations with incoming sensory information.
Anthony de MelloFamiliar labels can keep us from looking closely at what is in front of us.
Sam HarrisAn invitation to examine the feeling of being a separate observer of experience.
Jiddu KrishnamurtiQuestions about how learned habits shape thought.
Hanzi Freinacht and Ken WilberFrameworks for thinking about how people's perspectives change as they develop.

These sources include scientific models and philosophical interpretations. They approach different questions and do not establish a single explanation of the mind.

Beliefs as tools

In A New Way to Think, Mili explores using more than one perspective on a problem. A useful perspective can open up a choice, but usefulness alone does not make a belief true.

An everyday example

A short message might read as rude when you are tired and ordinary the next morning. Asking what else could explain it is a small way to check an interpretation before acting on it.

Life Philosophy of Beauty

Beauty & Daily Life
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Make room for attention, enjoyment, and care alongside achievement.

Life Philosophy of Beauty explores what makes a day worthwhile beyond getting things done. Mili uses beauty broadly: a good conversation, a graceful movement, or something made with care.

Beyond achievement

His writing asks what happens to our sense of purpose as AI can do more of the work people once measured themselves by. One response he explores is to give more attention to the experience of living, alongside what we produce.

Beauty in ordinary choices

  • Conversation: Leave room for the other person and follow what is interesting.
  • Sport: Enjoy the movement and the exchange as well as the score.
  • Work: Take care with a small detail someone else will encounter.

The guiding question is what would make the moment more thoughtful, enjoyable, or well made.

Making room for it

Constant worry and comparison can make free time hard to enjoy. This is where the idea connects with Letting Go. Mili also relates it to Friedrich Schiller's writing on play: acting with freedom and enjoyment.

Get Off the Feed

Attention & Technology
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Choose what you want to read or make before a feed chooses for you.

Get Off the Feed is Mili's reminder to spend less time scrolling by habit. His essay Skip the feed explores the difference between opening an app with a purpose and letting it decide how the next hour goes.

What gets lost

A feed offers a new subject every few seconds. Mili's concern is that this leaves less time to stay with a question, finish a book, or develop an idea of your own.

Posting and scrolling

He still uses social platforms to share writing and videos. The distinction he draws is about how he uses them: publishing something or talking to someone can be a deliberate choice, while checking the feed can become a reflex.

Practical alternatives

  • Decide what you want to look up before opening the app.
  • Keep books, essays, or longer videos you actually want to finish within reach.
  • Use site blockers when repeated checking interrupts work.
  • Where possible, choose who and what appears in your feed.

The useful question is whether the time went where you wanted it to go.

Education Should Teach the Inner Life

Education
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Make understanding thoughts, emotions, and attention part of learning.

Education Should Teach the Inner Life is an argument in Mili's writing that schools should give more time to understanding thoughts, feelings, and attention.

The question

In Thought should be a subject in school and Spiritual Health as a Subject in School, he asks why students spend so much time learning about the world and comparatively little learning how to handle their own mental habits.

What lessons might cover

  1. Recognizing thoughts: Notice the difference between an event and the story you tell about it.
  2. Handling emotions: Name a feeling and practice pausing before reacting.
  3. Paying attention: Learn to notice distraction and return to a task.
  4. Unstructured time: Leave room to discover interests without filling every pause.
  5. Choosing a direction: Explore what makes learning worthwhile beyond grades.

Why AI enters the discussion

As tools make answers easier to obtain, Mili argues that judgment and self-understanding deserve more attention in education. The proposal raises practical questions too: who teaches these skills, and how can schools make room for different beliefs?

Personal Energy

Work & Daily Life
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Pay attention to what leaves you rested, engaged, or drained.

Personal Energy is a lens Mili uses when deciding how to spend his time. A free afternoon is different from an afternoon when you also have the attention and enthusiasm to use it.

Questions worth asking

  • Which commitments feel worthwhile afterward, even when they are tiring?
  • Which ones mostly come from wanting approval?
  • Is this task draining because it matters and is difficult, or because something needs to change?
  • Would rest help more than another attempt to force concentration?

Time and attention

In On personal energy, he explores why scheduling every available hour can miss how work actually feels. Sleep, food, exercise, and breaks are part of the picture, alongside whether a project holds your interest.

A lens, not a score

Relationships and responsibilities cannot always be judged by how energized they leave you. The point is to notice patterns and make more deliberate choices, rather than expect every worthwhile activity to feel good immediately.

Metamodernism

Culture & Politics
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Take criticism seriously while still trying to build something worthwhile.

Metamodernism is a topic Mili has explored through Hanzi Freinacht's The Listening Society and Ken Wilber's work. These notes focus on the question of how to remain hopeful without ignoring what is wrong.

The basic contrast

  • Modernism: In this framing, confidence in progress and the possibility of improving society.
  • Postmodernism: Questioning confident stories, including whose interests they serve.
  • Metamodernism: Taking those criticisms seriously while remaining willing to commit to something.

This is a simplified introduction to the contrast used in these notes.

The Listening Society

Mili's essays explore Freinacht's proposal that politics should pay more attention to people's emotional lives and development, alongside material needs.

Other recurring questions concern how relationships shape a person, how perspectives change over time, and how institutions could support that growth. These are ideas to examine, rather than a settled ranking of people or cultures.

A visual metaphor

The golden spiral appears as an image of growth that keeps building on what came before.

Network State & Pop-Up Cities

Communities & Places
From Milipedia, the personal knowledge base of Maximilian Rehn
Summary: Notes on online communities that meet, live, and build things together.

A Network State, in Balaji Srinivasan's proposal, begins as an online community and aims to develop a physical presence and eventually political recognition. Pop-up cities are temporary gatherings where people live and work together. A pop-up city does not have to share the larger political ambition.

Living with the idea

Mili's notes draw on experiences with Zuzalu, Edge Esmeralda, and Network School. In Reflections on Pop-up Cities, he considers what makes these communities rewarding in practice.

  • Repeated encounters: Sharing a place makes it easier for a conversation to turn into a friendship or project.
  • Shared spaces: Kitchens, work areas, and walkable surroundings influence how often people meet.
  • Doing things together: Projects give a community something concrete to work on.
  • Daily life: Exercise, rest, and time outside matter alongside work.

Related reading

  • The Sovereign Individual — James Dale Davidson and Lord William Rees-Mogg
  • The Beginning of Infinity — David Deutsch
